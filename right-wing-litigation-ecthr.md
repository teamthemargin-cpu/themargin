## Summary

In recent years, conservative and religious groups across Europe and beyond have started using the European Court of Human Rights — long seen as a guardian of progressive rights — as a venue to push back against norms around gender equality, LGBTI+ rights, labour rights, sexual and reproductive rights, and religion-state relations.

This isn't happening by accident. It reflects a coordinated, cross-border strategy: networks of lawyers, advocacy groups, and religious organizations deliberately bringing cases to reshape how human rights are interpreted at the highest level.

Most commentary frames this as a simple clash: progressives defending rights, conservatives attacking them. This project argues that picture is too simple. These cases raise genuinely hard questions: Where does religious freedom end and discrimination begin? How much room should democratic societies give to communities with traditional values? Who gets to define what "European values" actually means?

Rather than treating religious conservative litigation as mere backlash or obstruction, this research takes it seriously as a form of engagement with human rights law — one that forces the court, and all of us, to grapple with what pluralism and fairness really require.

The core argument: the European Court of Human Rights is not a neutral arbiter handing down settled truths. It is a battleground where competing visions of rights, democracy, and European identity are actively being fought over — and the outcome will shape how human rights law develops for decades to come.

## Key findings

This project maps how conservative and religious networks are deliberately using the European Court of Human Rights to reshape human rights law — coordinating across borders, deploying sophisticated rights-based arguments, and exploiting the court's own doctrines as entry points.

Rather than treating this as simple backlash, the research reframes it as a proactive attempt to build an alternative vision of human rights — one that takes seriously the genuine tensions between religious freedom, gender equality, and LGBTI+ rights.

The core contribution is reconceiving the ECHR not as a neutral, progressively-trending institution, but as a genuinely open and contested arena where competing visions of rights, pluralism, and European identity are actively being fought over
