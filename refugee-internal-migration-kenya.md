## Summary

Across Kenya, many refugees are making a difficult but deliberate choice: leaving camps and moving to cities. This is not accidental mobility, it is a strategy for survival, dignity, and opportunity. Refugees are not just leaving camps, they are navigating systems that were not designed for them. If policy continues to ignore this reality, it will fail. But if we align policy with lived experience, we can transform displacement into opportunity for refugees and host communities alike.

## Academic abstract

Seventy-five years after the adoption of the 1951 Refugee Convention, the lived realities of refugees in host states show both its enduring relevance and its practical limits. Based on lived experience, the internal migration of refugees within Kenya, this paper examines how movement, settlement, and survival strategies illuminate critical gaps between the Convention's principles and everyday governance practices in Kenya. Through qualitative interviews, participant observation, and lived experience reflexivity, the research shows how bureaucratic procedures such as movement permits, inconsistent regulation, fragmented legal frameworks, and the selective implementation of refugee policies produce what the author calls "administrative negligence."

This negligence pushes refugees into patterns of secondary mobility that are essential for accessing safety and dignity, yet still framed as "irregular" by policymakers from the Global North. The paper argues that refugee mobility within a host state is not a violation of the international refugee regime or hosting country regulations, but an expression of agency that reflects the Convention's core purpose: enabling refugees to rebuild lives in safety. However, restrictive practices by the state contradict the Convention's spirit, even when formally aligned with domestic law.

By situating refugee movement within the political economy of Kenya's encampment model, the paper highlights how refugee camp-to-urban mobility within Kenya exposes structural tensions between protection and refugee autonomy.

## Key findings

Beyond its practical relevance, the paper contributes to academic debates from a human rights perspective by reframing refugee mobility as an expression of the right to seek dignity, livelihoods, and freedom of movement, rather than a deviation from policy. It highlights how encampment frameworks can constrain fundamental rights and exposes the gap between formal policy and lived realities, showing how refugees navigate these restrictions to access basic socio-economic rights and protection.
